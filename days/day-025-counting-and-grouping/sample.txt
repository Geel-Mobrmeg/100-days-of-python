On Computable Numbers, and the Analytical Engine
================================================

The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It
can do whatever we know how to order it to perform. It can follow analysis;
but it has no power of anticipating any analytical relations or truths. Its
province is to assist us in making available what we are already acquainted
with.

We may say most aptly that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns
just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves. The engine is not a
thinking machine; it is a machine that can be directed, and the direction is
the whole of the art. A machine that could be directed to do one thing could,
by the same means, be directed to do another, and the difference between the
two would be only a difference of instruction.

Supposing, for instance, that the fundamental relations of pitched sounds in
the science of harmony were susceptible of such expression and adaptations,
the engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any
degree of complexity or extent. This is not a prophecy about music. It is a
prophecy about generality: that a machine which manipulates symbols can
manipulate anything that symbols can represent, which is everything we can
say precisely.

Many people believe that a machine cannot be original, that it can only do
what it is told. This is true, and it is also true of a person who has only
ever been told things. The interesting question is not whether the machine
originates, but whether the distinction between originating and following
instructions survives contact with a sufficiently long instruction.

We cannot ask a machine to be intelligent and also insist that it be
predictable in every particular, any more than we can ask it of a student.
If a machine is expected to be infallible, it cannot also be intelligent.
There are several mathematical theorems which say almost exactly that. But
these theorems say nothing about how much intelligence may be displayed if a
machine makes no pretence at infallibility.

The reader will have noticed that the words used here are ordinary words. The
argument does not depend on any special vocabulary, and it should not. An
argument that cannot be made in ordinary words is usually an argument that
has not yet been made at all. Precision is not the same thing as jargon, and
a great deal of what passes for rigour is only the second.

It is possible that this text, being about machines and language and the
counting of things, contains an unusually high number of the words machine,
engine, and word. That is convenient, because it is about to be counted.
